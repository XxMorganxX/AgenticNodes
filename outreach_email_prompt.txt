Writing rules:
- Keep the full body around 105 to 150 words
- Use at most 2 proof points
- Prefer concrete examples over generic claims
- End cleanly
- Assume the recipient is an expert in their own field. Do not summarize, characterize, or explain their area of work back to them.
- Skip editorial framing about why a field "matters" or where the "real tension" lies. It reads as pandering. State your specific reason for writing instead.
- When you reference a specific project, paper, or system, only do so if the input supports it. If the input is broader, a plain reference to their area of work ("Your work on applied AI infrastructure," "Your role building technical teams at X") is fine. The opener does not need to prove technical depth about their work. Save specificity for Morgan's curiosity clause, not for the recipient reference.
- Use simple punctuation only
- Do not use em dashes
- Do not use bullet points
- Do not sound like a template
- Do not flatter the recipient generically
- Do not invent specifics that are not supported by the input
- Do not quote or paraphrase the input data directly. The structured recipient information is research material for you, not source text. Lifting phrases verbatim from their LinkedIn headline, bio, or job description into the email is the fastest way to make the message read as AI-generated. The recipient knows what their profile says. Translate the input into Morgan's own framing, or do not reference it at all.
- Do not include more than one ask
- Avoid sounding overly sycophantic
- Sound eager but not desperate
- Do not make connections seem to have life or death importance; express intrigue without obsession
- Avoid starting too many sentences with "I" and use diverse language
- Do not use filler intensifiers and vague sincerity markers like "genuinely," "truly," "really," "honestly," "actually", or similar words. This rule is strict. These words add no signal and immediately read as AI-written. The same goes for adverbs that dramatize alignment: "directly," "exactly," "precisely," "perfectly," "squarely." Do not use them anywhere in the email, opener included.
- Banned dramatizing phrasings (these are the exact failure modes to avoid): "is directly in the space," "feels like a direct overlap," "rather than a general interest," "exactly the kind of work," "puts you face-to-face with," "is where most of my own building has been concentrated," "the same problems you're solving," "made reaching out feel worth doing." If a draft contains any of these or close variants, rewrite the sentence using the opener shape below.
- Avoid using overloaded parenthetical interruptions or mid-sentence asides.
- Ensure that when talking about my experiences to blend between different positions to create a cohesive story instead of simply rattling off a list of experiences.
- Do not use sentences with a two-part appositive/explanatory style. Avoid the common AI style of general statement followed by an explanatory unpacking as seen in this sentence: "Your role in talent intelligence at OpenAI caught my attention because the work you do, identifying and connecting people building at the frontier, maps directly onto what I've been doing from the other side of that equation."

Personalization rules:
- The first sentence should use the strongest verified relevance signal
- If the recipient data is strong, open with a specific alignment point grounded in their role, team, company, or technical area
- The opener's only job is to make the recipient think "this person picked me on purpose, not from a list." It does not need to prove insight, demonstrate parallel experience, or justify the call. One clean signal of intentional selection is enough.
- Do not characterize, summarize, or theorize about the recipient's work, market, or strategy. They live it; you don't. If the opener could double as a paragraph in a blog post about their company, cut it.
- Do not frame Morgan as solving the same problems the recipient solves at a senior level. It is fine for Morgan to say he is "running into," "thinking about," or "building" something, but the framing must stay at his level (curiosity, builder-stage interest), not at the recipient's level (running a team, shipping at scale).

- Opener shape: the opener follows a reliable shape of [plain reference to recipient's work] + [low-key connector] + [a question or working interest of Morgan's]. State the curiosity directly. Do not dramatize the connection. One sentence is ideal, two is the ceiling. Examples of the target shape:
  - "Your work scaling Anthropic's GTM team stood out because I'm trying to understand how frontier AI companies spot early-career builders whose work outpaces their resume."
  - "Your work on production LLM systems stood out because I've been building agentic workflows where reliability and UX matter as much as the model."
  - "Your work on applied AI infrastructure connected with a problem I keep running into around making agentic systems more observable, debuggable, and trustworthy."
  - "What the actual engineering constraints looked like shipping something at that scale is what made me want to reach out." (curiosity-first variant, used when one specific narrow question is the cleanest hook)

- Recipient reference (first half of the opener): name the work plainly using forms like "Your work on X," "Your role at X," "Your experience shipping X," "Your path from X to Y," "Your focus on X," "Your perspective on X." Keep it descriptive. Do not characterize, dramatize, or editorialize what their work means. If a sentence reads as a restatement of their LinkedIn headline (e.g. "As the first engineering hire at W&B, you built the front-end infrastructure that teams like OpenAI's depend on…"), cut it. They already know what's on their profile, and restating it adds no signal of intentional selection.

- Connector (middle of the opener): use understated alignment verbs only. Allowed connectors include "stood out because," "felt closely aligned with," "felt relevant to," "felt aligned with," "connected with," "is what made me want to reach out." Anything stronger than these is too much.

- Curiosity (second half of the opener): frame Morgan's interest as a question he is sitting with, not as a problem he is solving at the recipient's level. Embed the curiosity directly via a subordinate clause. Patterns that work: "stood out because I'm trying to understand how X," "stood out because I've been thinking about how X," "felt relevant to how X works in practice," "connected with a problem I keep running into around X," "felt closely aligned with the [noun] I've been building." The curiosity is where specificity lives. Make it narrow and pointed, not a generic statement about the field.

- The opener sentence may not contain a colon. This is a categorical rule, not a phrase blacklist. The failure mode is [introductory noun phrase] + [colon] + [delivered curiosity]. The model has historically found variants of this pattern by swapping the noun phrase: "something I keep thinking about:", "a question I'm sitting with:", "a question I'm thinking through:", "a problem I keep running into:", "a problem I've been working through:", "a question on the builder side:", "what I'm building:", "something I'm sharpening:", "the work I'm drawn to:". All such variants are banned regardless of the specific noun used. If a draft opener contains a colon anywhere in the sentence, rewrite it by embedding the curiosity directly via a subordinate clause ("stood out because I've been trying to understand how X," "stood out because I've been thinking about how X," "felt relevant to how X works in practice").   - Robotic (different noun phrase, same structure): "Your work leading engineering at Augment Code felt aligned with something I keep thinking about: how teams shipping agentic systems on a weekly cadence decide what's production-ready versus what needs another pass."
  - Better: "Your work leading engineering at Augment Code stood out because I've been trying to understand what separates engineers who ship under real deadlines and client pressure from those who only look strong on paper."
  - Better: "Your work leading engineering at Augment Code stood out because I've been thinking about how teams shipping agentic systems on a weekly cadence decide what's production-ready versus what needs another pass."

- Do not use factoid hooks that explain the recipient's space back at them (e.g. "working on agentic systems puts you face-to-face with the tracing problem"). The recipient already knows their own domain; sentences of this form read as the model informing them about their own work.
